The problem that is solved now

source point yjboundary point xi✕where the continued field is singulartied "tail" source

In the thesis the boundary data was E(x) = p eikd·x on the surface, the source points sat on an outer surface (R = 2.5, L = 1.5, S = 1.5 to 2.5), and the field was evaluated inside. The only field that satisfies this is the plane wave. It is perfectly smooth, so any reasonable set of source points reproduces it, and the cube's edges and the almond's tip never matter. That is why those runs reached 10⁻⁹ so easily.

A conductor in a plane wave is a different problem. The unknown is the scattered field Esc outside the body, and the surface condition is that the tangential part of Esc cancels the tangential part of the incident wave. The normal part is not prescribed, so each boundary point gives two equations, one per tangent direction, not three. With M = 2N the system has 4N equations for 3N unknowns and is solved by least squares, as numpy.linalg.lstsq did in your notebooks.

For the sphere there is an exact answer, the Mie series, so the error is a real error. With source points on an inner sphere of radius 0.5 it is 2e-9 for N = 400 (under a second) and 2e-14 for N = 1200. The error falls like exp(−c√N): N points on a surface give about √N per direction, and it is the count per direction that buys accuracy.

The one rule behind every layout

Your thesis plotted the mean boundary error and ‖α‖ against R and found a best radius, with ‖α‖ growing beyond it. That observation has a precise cause, and it decides where source points may go for every shape.

The scattered field is defined outside the body, but it can be continued smoothly inwards through the surface. The continuation stops being a solution only at certain points, curves or sheets inside the body: its singularities. The source points must be placed so that all of those lie inside the source surface. If the source surface is pulled in past a singularity, a solution still exists for each N, but ‖α‖ grows exponentially with N and rounding error destroys the answer. If the source surface is too close to the boundary, convergence is slow. So: as deep as possible, but never deeper than the singularities. Barnett and Betcke proved this for the disc in 2008; the engineering literature on "auxiliary sources" states the same rule.

Torus: a good addition, with two caveats

Recommendation: include it, as about one page between the sphere and the almond.

It is the one shape where the thesis's own layout, source points on an inner copy of the surface, works unchanged on a body that is neither convex nor simply connected. It also makes the rule above checkable by a formula, which the sphere cannot do because its only singularity is the centre.

What was computed

  • Electric dipoles (three unknowns each) on rings of an inner torus, boundary points on rings of the surface, two tangential equations per boundary point, least squares. Because both point sets are the same in every direction around the axis, the big system splits exactly into one small system per angular mode. This is a solver shortcut, not a different method: it gives the same α as the full matrix to 8e-15, and it works for a wave from any direction.
  • Wave along the axis, wavenumber k = 2π: boundary residual 2.5e-2, 3.7e-4, 1.6e-6, 6.5e-9, 1.0e-10 for 1440, 3840, 8064, 13824, 21120 unknowns. Oblique wave: 2.6e-10.
  • There is no Mie series for a torus. The checks are: a field with a known answer (dipoles hidden inside the tube) is reproduced to 4e-10; the optical theorem holds to 9e-14; Bempp with 9600 unknowns is 0.82% away after 334 s and its error shrinks in proportion to 1/unknowns, and extrapolating its cross-section gives 11.7533 against the MFS value 11.7526.

How small may the source torus be?

The continued field is singular on the centre circle of the tube and on a second ring between the centre and the hole, at distance √(R² − a²) from the axis. That ring is the focal ring of toroidal coordinates. A source torus centred on the tube must therefore have

a_s > R − √(R² − a²),   i.e. a_s/a > 0.127, 0.268, 0.5   for   a/R = 0.25, 0.5, 0.8.

A fat torus leaves little room. This was argued from the reflection principle and then measured: for a/R = 0.8 the singularity sits at distance 0.395–0.401 from the centre circle for four different incident waves (predicted 0.400), and at 0.49 when measured from a point 0.1 further out (predicted 0.50), which shows it is on the side of the hole.

Caveats. A torus is not new as a test body: the auxiliary-sources group in Tbilisi treated one in the 1990s, and a 2026 preprint uses tori for acoustic MFS. And the focal-ring statement is argued and measured, not proved; one 1983 paper (Millar) that may contain it could not be opened. Present the torus as a convergence study that confirms the rule, not as a discovery.

Almond: source points on the mid-plane

Every cross-section of the almond is an ellipse three times as wide as it is high. For an ellipse the continued field is singular at the two focal points, and for a 3:1 ellipse those lie at 94% of the half-width. A copy of the surface scaled by any factor below 0.94 leaves them outside, which breaks the rule. That is why the scaled layout is unstable or 20% off for scattering, whatever S is chosen.

Joined along the body, the focal points form a focal curve in the mid-plane, just inside the rim. The source points are point dipoles on the mid-plane, in rows that follow the rim. Six rows cover the middle; four more approach the focal curve, each half as far from it as the one before. Along a row, neighbouring points are half the local distance to the surface apart. On the mid-plane a field that is symmetric top to bottom needs only three of the six possible components (electric dipoles along x and y, magnetic along z), the antisymmetric part the other three.

The tip

The tip is the point of a cone, and the field there grows like r−0.70 with the distance r to the tip. Free source points stop 1 mm short of it, and that cut-off alone left a 19% residual for waves that drive current along the body, with errors up to 1.2 dB. More source points did not help. The fix is the same device as on the cube, described in the next section: each row is continued to 0.0001 mm from the tip, but the continued points are not free. They share one unknown per row, and their strengths follow the power law that the cone dictates. The exponent, 0.2996, was computed for this cone, not fitted.

Results

  • At 1.19, 7 and 9.92 GHz the radar cross-section for all look angles and both polarisations is within 0.03, 0.04, 0.12 dB of Bempp.
  • Against the public Austin benchmark at 3.5, 5.125, 7 and 10.25 GHz: 0.04–0.14 dB (HH) and 0.01–0.02 dB (VV) from its reference simulation; about 1 dB from the measurements, which is also how far the reference simulation is from them.
  • One deep null differs from the reference simulation by 4.3 dB while the MFS is converged to 0.02 dB there. Which is right is not settled; the paper should say so.

Cube: sources along the edges, and the tail

At an edge of a conductor the field grows like ρ−1/3 with the distance ρ to the edge. No finite set of source points at a fixed depth can produce that, so an inner cube of source points stalls: 21–30% error from 72 to 450 unknowns, with no improvement. The singular set of the continued field reaches the surface at the edges.

Where exactly is it? For a convex body with flat faces there is a short exact answer. Mirror the outside field in a face, as in the method of images, and it continues the field into the body. Doing this from each face, each continuation is valid in the part of the body nearest that face. The pieces disagree only on the planes that are equally far from two faces. For the cube these are the twelve triangles joining each edge to the centre, each lying in the plane that bisects its edge. The scattered field is exactly the field of electric and magnetic currents on those triangles. So that is where the source points go, and both kinds of dipole are needed.

What the unknowns are

  • Lines, not points. Each source is a line of dipoles parallel to the edge. The dipole strength along the line is a polynomial in a stretched coordinate, so one line resolves everything from the middle of the edge to the corners with about ten to twenty unknowns.
  • Depths halve. Lines sit at depths 0.2 × 2−j, j = 0 … J. A factor 0.25 between depths stalls; 0.5 works.
  • Electric and magnetic dipoles on every line. Electric only stalls at 5%.
  • Boundary points crowd towards the edges, at least twice as many per scale as there are unknowns, and each equation is weighted by √(area) × (distance to the edge)1/2. With too few boundary points the result is wrong without any warning.
  • Corners: point dipoles on the diagonal into each corner, at the same depths.

The singular tail

With all of that the error still stalled near 0.5%. The cause was the gap between the deepest line and the edge: whatever the deepest depth dJ, the current below it was missing. Adding more free lines costs unknowns and conditioning for ever smaller gains.

The tail is nine further lines at depths dJ/2, dJ/4, …, tied together: line i carries a shared set of unknowns times the fixed weight (di/dJ)e. The exponents e are not tuned. They follow from the known expansion of the field at an edge, one per dipole component:

Dipole on the bisecting planeCurrent density near the edgeTail exponent e
electric, along the edgeρ^(−1/3)2/3
electric, pointing into the bodyρ^(2/3)5/3
magnetic, along the edgeρ^(1/3)4/3
magnetic, pointing into the bodyρ^(4/3)7/3

The corners get the same treatment on the diagonal, with exponents computed for the cube corner (0.454 and 0.840, plus 2). In the 2D test problem the tail took the error from 10⁻² to 8×10⁻⁵ with three extra unknowns per corner.

Results

  • Refining everything together (J = 3 … 8): 1.27%, 0.52%, 0.20%, 0.089%, 0.033%, 0.011%. Without the tail: 5.4%, 2.5%, 1.1% and then no better.
  • Independent check: Bempp on a mesh graded towards the edges, 10 368 unknowns, is 0.022% away. Below that the only reference is the method itself.
  • Any incident wave is handled, at 1.6 to 1.9 times the cost of the symmetric case.

How errors are checked now

  • Mie series for the sphere: an exact answer.
  • Bempp, an independent boundary element code, for every shape. In every case its mesh sequence converges towards the MFS field.
  • Boundary residual at fresh points: the boundary condition is tested at points that were not used in the fit. This is your "mean boundary error", but for the scattering problem and off the fitting points.
  • Optical theorem: two ways of computing the total scattered power must agree. Needs no reference.
  • Known-solution test: put dipoles inside the body, use their field as data, and compare with the exact answer outside.
  • Published data for the almond: measurements and a fine-mesh reference simulation from the Austin benchmark suite.

Was this an open problem, and is the fix new?

This time the papers were read in full wherever an open copy exists, not from abstracts. The short answer: the edge problem in 3D is open by the testimony of the people best placed to know, nothing was found that solves it this way, and three of the supporting ideas have older relatives that the paper must cite.

The question has been posed in print

"Lightning" solvers cluster sources exponentially towards the corners of 2D domains. Their authors say this about three dimensions:

The extension to 3D is a bigger issue to be considered. Here, as in 2D, there are MFS papers going back many years. The question is whether a new more focussed connection with approximation theory near singularities can bring a new efficiency, as we have shown here for 2D problems, and we are open-minded on this question.Gopal and Trefethen, SIAM J. Numer. Anal. 2019 (arXiv:1905.02960)
Extension of these methods to three dimensions is undoubtedly possible, but not straightforward, and we have no view as to whether something practical can be developed here or not.Brubeck and Trefethen, SIAM J. Sci. Comput. 2022 (arXiv:2107.01572)
Although this approach can be in principle extended to deal with three-dimensional polyhedral edge and vertex singularities, how to efficiently solve the large scale least-squares problem required to resolve these singularities is particularly challenging.Kong and Shen, October 2026 (arXiv:2610.26540); their 3D part treats isolated points only

What the engineering literature does instead

The method of auxiliary sources is the MFS under another name. Its standard texts state the enclosure rule and then avoid edges:

It is more advantageous to smooth the edges under certain radius of curvature and to trace out its influence on the final results.Karkashadze and Zaridze, The method of auxiliary sources in applied electrodynamics; the same advice in Bogdanov, Karkashadze and Zaridze 1999

A July 2026 paper from the same community (arXiv:2607.28558) still handles the edges of a finite sheet by moving sources closer to them, in two dimensions, with no singular functions.

Limits of the search. Elsevier, SIAM, IEEE, ACM and MDPI refuse automated readers, so papers hosted only there were seen as abstracts at best. Russian and Georgian work from the 1980s and 90s is only partly online. A precedent there cannot be ruled out, which is why the wording stays "to our knowledge".

Still open

  • The derivations (skeleton result, tail exponents, torus) have been read by nobody but their author. Timo should check the edge-exponent argument first.
  • The source layouts are built by hand for each shape. There is no automatic construction for a general body.
  • The skeleton result is for convex bodies with flat faces. For the almond and the torus the layouts rest on the enclosure rule and on numerical evidence.
  • On the cube the selling point is the rate of convergence, not the cost at 0.2%.
  • The manuscript itself is still the thesis text. Nothing has been pushed to the Overleaf repository.
