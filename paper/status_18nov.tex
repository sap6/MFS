\textbf{1. What changed in substance}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Same symmetry in both codes, everywhere.} Cube: three mirror planes and the rotation about the body diagonal. Almond: two mirror planes. Torus: the rotational symmetry. I had written that Bempp cannot use the rotation; it can, because its torus mesh repeats around the axis, and I built it. Sphere: neither code uses any.
\item \textbf{Three wavelengths now converges.} The paper's cube layout works unchanged with twelve more modes per line. The ``limit'' reported before came from an earlier session that had scaled the first source depth with the wavelength.
\item \textbf{What the theorem shows.} An independent reading pointed out that the theorem does not say the field is singular on the whole skeleton. The continuation through one face is regular inside the cube; the singular points are the edges and vertices. The theorem shows where sources \emph{can} be placed. Abstract, introduction, Sections 4 and 5 and the conclusion now say this. The theorem and its proof are unchanged.
\item \textbf{A wrong sentence removed.} ``Adding the next term changes the error by less than 1\%, lowering two exponents gives 65\%'' came from an old layout. With the paper's layout: 3 to 4\%, and 4 to 10 times larger.
\item \textbf{Bempp's finest mesh.} The paper said Bempp stops at 28800 unknowns for lack of memory. With the leaner symmetry code 41472 fit, so that mesh is now in all four cube cases. Bempp's smallest errors improve; the MFS still reaches a smaller error in half of Bempp's time or less.
\item \textbf{Comparisons are stated by actual runs}, not by interpolating between them, and the cube comparison is now one small table (Table 5).
\item \textbf{Almond at a second frequency.} At 3.5 GHz (three wavelengths long) the picture is the same: MFS $4\times10^{-5}$ in 4 s, Bempp $1\times10^{-3}$ in half a minute.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{3. A shape that is hard to mesh but easy for the MFS}

I found none, in the literature or by trying. A wavy torus that integral-equation papers use as a test shape is \emph{hard} for the plain MFS (5\% error after a minute). What mesh-free buys is exact geometry and very fast convergence on smooth bodies whose singularities lie deep inside. The torus row above is that case.
